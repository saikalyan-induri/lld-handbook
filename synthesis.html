Cross-Problem Synthesis Problems 1–20

This section spans all twenty problems in this document. The same handful of lessons recur throughout — the value of working through this many problems isn't twenty independent tricks, it's watching a small set of ideas (check-then-act races, idempotent retries, template-vs-snapshot splits, and a short list of patterns) show up again and again in different clothing.

Recurring Interview Signals Across All Twenty

  • The check-then-act race is the single most repeated concurrency bug shape in this entire document — Parking Lot's spot occupation, Ride-Sharing's driver reservation, Coffee Machine's ingredient reservation, ATM's cross-channel balance check, Library's last-copy checkout, Hotel's overlapping-date booking, Movie Ticket Booking's seat hold, and E-commerce's inventory decrement are the same bug in eight different domains, at wildly different scales. Naming "atomic compare-and-swap, not read-then-write" unprompted, and recognizing it as the same bug every time it recurs, is the single most transferable signal across all twenty problems.
  • The atomic select-then-claim race is a specific, recurring variant of the above, for dispatch/assignment problems — Elevator's hall-call assignment and Food Delivery's courier assignment are the same bug: selecting a candidate and claiming them must be one atomic step, not two, or two requests can select the same "available" resource before either claim commits.
  • Deadlock avoidance via canonical lock ordering is the recurring fix whenever a single operation must lock multiple resources at once — File System's cross-directory move, Movie Ticket Booking's multi-seat hold, and E-commerce's multi-item checkout all resolve the same circular-wait risk the same way: sort the resource ids and always acquire locks in that order, so every concurrent request converges on one global order.
  • Idempotency under retries is the second most repeated correctness requirement — Splitwise's settle-up, Ride-Sharing's ride-accept, Notification System's dedupe, Pub-Sub's at-least-once delivery, Movie Ticket Booking's and E-commerce's payment-webhook confirmation, Food Delivery's duplicate status events, and Chat's cursor-based reconnect replay are eight instances of exactly the same lesson: once retries exist, "processed twice" is not an edge case, it's an expected event the design must tolerate, ideally by construction (a cursor, an idempotency key) rather than by a bolted-on duplicate check.
  • A recurring "shared, mutable template vs. frozen, per-instance snapshot" split is the most common non-obvious modeling decision in the second half of this document — Library's Book/BookItem, Hotel's Room/RoomInventory, Movie Ticket Booking's Seat/ShowSeat, Food Delivery's MenuItem/OrderLineItem, and E-commerce's Cart/Order are five independent instances of the identical shape: a catalog/template entity that's shared and changes over time, versus a specific instance or historical record that must stay frozen once created. Collapsing the two into one entity is the most common and most damaging shortcut across these five problems specifically.
  • Deliberately naming and rejecting a plausible-looking pattern is a stronger signal than reflexively applying one — this appears explicitly in eight of the twenty problems (Splitwise's Payment-not-a-subclass, Ride-Sharing's Decorator-over-second-Strategy, Notification System's rejected Bridge, Coffee Machine's rejected Template Method, Pub-Sub's rejected Bridge, Elevator's rejected Singleton, Chess's rejected Strategy for piece movement, E-commerce's Decorator/Strategy role-swap rejection). The consistent shape of the argument — "this pattern's structural fit is real, but its intent doesn't match what's actually varying here" — is more valuable to demonstrate than the specific pattern each time.
  • Strategy is the default answer whenever the interviewer says "what if this needs to support X differently" — but three other patterns mark its real boundaries: Decorator is for composable, stackable modifiers (Ride-Sharing's surge, E-commerce's add-ons) rather than mutually exclusive alternatives; Visitor is for a growing set of read-only operations over a fixed structure (File System); Command is for actions that must themselves be queued, logged, or reversed (Chess's Move/undo). E-commerce (Problem 19) is the one place in this document where the Strategy/Decorator boundary is made fully explicit as the central lesson of the entire problem.
  • Singleton is a naive shortcut almost everywhere it appears in reference solutions — except when it isn't — Logging Framework's registry is the one legitimate case in this document, and Elevator's Dispatcher is a second, independent instance of the naive shortcut being tempting and wrong for exactly the same reason Parking Lot's and Ride-Sharing's service classes were. Stating explicitly why the registry case differs is more valuable than either reflexively using or reflexively banning Singleton.
  • Observer starts as an optional hook and ends as the structural core of an entire product — twice — the notify-interested-parties idea appears as a small, optional instance in Ride-Sharing, Notification System, Coffee Machine, Library, Hotel, Movie Ticket Booking, and Food Delivery, gets generalized fully as the entire point of Pub-Sub (Problem 10), and then converges a second time as the structural core of Chat (Problem 20) — arguably the most natural full-scale realization of exactly what Pub-Sub generalized. Recognizing this progression across ten separate problems demonstrates the pattern is understood structurally, not memorized per-problem.
  • Mediator and Observer can coexist on the same class, solving different problems — Chat's Conversation is simultaneously a Mediator (decoupling participants from direct references to each other) and an Observer subject (fanning out messages to connected clients). Recognizing that these are complementary lenses, not competing pattern choices, is the clearest illustration in this document that pattern selection isn't always "pick exactly one."
  • Recognizing the LLD/HLD boundary explicitly (clearest in Ride-Sharing, Pub-Sub, Movie Ticket Booking, and Chat, but present in every problem once "scale this to millions of users" or "run this across a cluster" comes up) separates strong candidates from those who either over-engineer infrastructure they weren't asked for or hand-wave scale entirely.
